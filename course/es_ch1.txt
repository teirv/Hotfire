=U ssound | Sounds of Spanish | Spanish spelling is regular, so learn the sounds once.
=L ss1 | Vowels and letters
@T
Spanish is almost always spoken the way it's written. Tap any Spanish word to hear it.

The five vowels never change: `a` as in father, `e` as in hey, `i` as in machine, `o` as in go, `u` as in rule.

Say them short and clean: `casa`, `mesa`, `sí`, `solo`, `luna`.
@T
Letters that differ from English:

`h` is silent: `hola`

`j` is a breathy h: `jefe`

`ñ` sounds like ny in canyon: `año`

`ll` and `y` sound like y in yes: `llave`, `yo`

`rr` is a rolled r: `perro`

`v` sounds like b: `vino`

`qu` sounds like k: `queso`

`z`, and `c` before e or i, sound like s in Latin America: `zapato`, `cena`

`g` before e or i sounds like j: `gente`
@Q
What's special about the h in `hola`?
~opts
* It's silent
- It sounds like English h
- It sounds like j
~why
Spanish h makes no sound at all.
@Q
Which word has a sound like the ny in canyon?
~opts
* `niño`
- `nido`
- `nota`
~why
ñ is its own letter.
@W
How do you spell the Spanish word for yes? It has an accent mark.
~accept
sí
~why
`si` without the accent means if.
=L ss2 | Stress and accent marks
@T
Which syllable gets stressed follows simple rules.

Words ending in a vowel, n, or s stress the second-to-last syllable: `casa`, `hablan`.

Other words stress the last syllable: `hablar`, `ciudad`.

A written accent breaks the rule: `teléfono`, `café`.

Accents can also change meaning: `el` the, `él` he.

Questions and exclamations open with upside-down marks: `¿Cómo estás?`, `¡Hola!`
@Q
Where's the stress in `hablo`?
~opts
* On ha
- On blo
- Equal on both
~why
It ends in a vowel, so the second-to-last syllable is stressed.
@Q
Which mark opens a question in Spanish?
~opts
* `¿`
- `¡`
- `?`
~why
`¡` opens an exclamation.
@Q
What's the difference between `el` and `él`?
~opts
* The and he
- He and she
- There's no difference
~why
The accent changes the meaning here.

=U sbasic | Building Sentences | Gender, articles and the verb to be.
=L sb1 | Nouns and articles
@T
Every Spanish noun is **masculine** or **feminine**. Most ending in `-o` are masculine, and most ending in `-a` are feminine.

The: `el libro`, `la casa`, `los libros`, `las casas`

A, some: `un libro`, `una casa`, `unos libros`, `unas casas`

Plurals add `-s` after a vowel, and `-es` after a consonant: `ciudades`.

Exceptions to learn: `el día`, `el problema`, `la mano`.
@Q
Which article goes with `mesa`?
~opts
* `la`
- `el`
- `los`
~why
It ends in -a, so it's feminine.
@Q
What's the plural of `el amigo`?
~opts
* `los amigos`
- `las amigas`
- `el amigos`
~why
The article and the noun both change.
@B
Say "a house."
~code
___ casa
~opts
* una
- un
- la
~why
Feminine "a" is `una`.
=L sb2 | Pronouns and ser
@T
Subject pronouns: `yo` I, `tú` you, `él` he, `ella` she, `usted` you (formal), `nosotros` we, `ustedes` you all, `ellos` they.

Spain also uses `vosotros` for "you all". Latin America doesn't.

**ser** (to be) is for identity, jobs, origin, and traits:

`soy`, `eres`, `es`, `somos`, `son`

`Soy mecánico`: I'm a mechanic. No "a" before jobs.

You can usually drop the pronoun, since the verb shows who.

~opts

- Son
- Soy
~why
`nosotros` goes with `somos`.

~opts

- He's an engineer
- She was an engineer
~why
`ella` is she.
@W

~accept

~why
`tú eres`.
=L sb3 | Estar: the other to be

Compare: `Es aburrido` means he's boring. `Está aburrido` means he's bored.

=L sb4 | Adjectives and asking questions
@T
Adjectives come **after** the noun and match it: `el coche rojo`, `la casa roja`, `los coches rojos`.

Those ending in `-e` or a consonant only change for plural: `grande`, `grandes`.

Question words all carry accents: `qué` what, `quién` who, `dónde` where, `cuándo` when, `por qué` why, `cómo` how, `cuánto` how much.

`Hay` means there is, or there are: `Hay un problema`.

=L sv2 | Tener, ir, hacer

`tener` (have): `tengo`, `tienes`, `tiene`, `tenemos`, `tienen`

`ir` (go): `voy`, `vas`, `va`, `vamos`, `van`

=L sv3 | Stem changes

Others: `empezar`, `volver`, `servir`, `jugar` (`juego`).

=L sv4 | Gustar: what you like
@T
`gustar` works backward: something is pleasing to you.

`Me gusta el café`: I like coffee

`Me gustan los perros`: I like dogs

Use `gusta` with one thing or a verb, and `gustan` with many.

`me` to me, `te` to you, `le` to him or her, `nos` to us, `les` to them

`¿Te gusta bailar?`: do you like to dance?

- gusta
- gusto
~why
Tacos are plural.
@Q

~opts
* We like to travel
- They like to travel
- You like to travel
~why
`nos` means to us.
